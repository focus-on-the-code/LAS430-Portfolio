Priority 1: Prof. T's Writing Expectations

Course artifact — adapted for public review from Canvas export

The TLDR: My expectations for your writing are detailed below. You are expected to know, and apply this guidance to your writing. You will not be successful if you stop at this summary.  

  • Be clear, correct, and original in your writing. No plagiarism. Always cite sources in MLA format. 
  • Your voice matters. I want your ideas, not copy-pastes. 
  • Effort shows. Each draft should improve. Use my feedback, available writing tools, and your judgment. 

The full document below explains: 

  • How to quote, paraphrase, summarize, and synthesize without plagiarizing. 
  • What to avoid (run on sentences, cliches, filler phrases). 
  • Stylistic choices that work, and ones that don’t, in academic writing. 
  • Common grammar traps that will cost you clarity. 

Philosophy & Context  

This is a 400-level course with written, audio/video and slide deck presentations submitted as assignments. I expect that all of your submissions are well designed and delivered: zero plagiarism, without spelling errors, grammatically correct, with accurate MLA citations and Works Cited list/pages, and the necessary amount of rehearsal where required. I also expect your work to be creative and insightful, expressing your unique ideas and analytical and creative styles. I don’t expect your work to be perfect. I do expect you to put forth the effort to continuously improve.   

Your ability to clearly express yourself is essential, no matter your profession. But the style of expression, and the audience’s expectations will change with every job, medium, and person. Your challenge will be to understand the expectations of your audience (myself and your classmates), and align your content and voice to deliver on their expectations.   

Your primary focus in this class is to create thoughtful, engaging content through the completion of your assignments. Content that presents your original ideas, speaks with your voice, and meets the requirements of the assignment. Content that is clear, well organized, and understandable by your audience – not just me, but the whole class. I look forward to seeing your ideas take shape, and your work improve through our time working together.   

The information and resources below will guide you to your best work. This is not an exhaustive list, and not a rule book. My intent is to highlight common issues and give you my perspective on alternatives that may enhance your work. For more support, check out the resources in the Siena Heights University Online Writing Center (OWL).Links to an external site.   

Use this guidance, my feedback, and your judgment (and your digital spell check and grammar check tools), and your writing will improve with every assignment.  

-Prof. T  

Quoting, Paraphrasing, Summarizing, and Synthesizing: How to Avoid Plagiarism  

Quoting, paraphrasing, summarizing, and synthesizing are all tools for working with other people’s ideas in your writing. Each one uses outside sources to strengthen your work, but do so in different ways (Moore; Beckwith):  

  • Quoting borrows the exact words of the source.  
  • Paraphrasing rewords the content, significantly changing the sentences and structure, while maintaining the core idea.  
  • Summarizing condenses the idea to just the essentials.  
  • Synthesizing connects multiple ideas and sources into something new. 

The difference comes down to how much of the source you keep, how closely you stick to the original wording, and how much of your own voice and thinking you bring in. 

No matter which method you use, you must give credit to the original source. Failing to do so, even if you changed the words and/or word-order, is plagiarism. As per Siena Heights University's (SHU) academic dishonesty policyLinks to an external site.:   

Plagiarism is the failure to give credit for the use of any material from outside sources, including the internet. It includes, but is not limited to: verbatim use of a quote without quotation marks and adequate documentation; submission of a paper, prepared by another person, as one’s own work; using the ideas, facts, words, photographs, pictures, graphics, or data of someone else and claiming them as your own; or not documenting ideas, facts, words or data gathered during research.  

To avoid plagiarism:  

  • Cite every source: whether you’re quoting, paraphrasing, summarizing, or synthesizing. Cite your sources both in-text, and in a Works Cited list/page. 
  • Use your own words and structure unless you’re quoting directly.  
  • Mark quotes clearly with quotation marks, ensure they are exact, and follow them with proper in-text citation.  
  • Understand the source before you write: copying and rearranging words is not paraphrasing, it’s plagiarism.  
  • Blend sources with your own analysis so your paper reflects your thinking, not just what others have said. 

  

Run On Sentences  

When two or more complete sentences are joined together without proper punctuation or a connecting word (e.g.: and, but, or). It’s not just about the sentence being too long. It’s about the clauses not being correctly separated or linked. For example:   

  • Fused sentence – Clauses run together with no punctuation.  
    • The sentence: I love reading it helps me relax.  
    • Should read: I love reading. It helps me relax. OR: I love reading, and it helps me relax.   
  • Comma splice – Clauses are joined only by a comma without a coordinating conjunction.  
    • The sentence: I love reading, it helps me relax.  
    • Should read: I love reading. It helps me relax. OR: I love reading, and it helps me relax.  

Why not add a semicolon (‘;’)? It’s A Trap! Correct use requires nuance, and restraint. They’re easy to overuse; use incorrectly; are confusing to the reader; and mask run on sentences, like this sentence. Instead: use a period, and start a new sentence. 

  

Cliches and Common Tropes  

Avoid common, unnecessary filler phrases. They are overused, non-specific, and add little value. Just strike it, and start with the very next word. Trust me,i It works more often than you think.  

Here's a few examples: 

  • This paper will / For this assignment  
  • In conclusion / At the end of the day  
  • Needless to say / It goes without saying  
  • In modern times / In the modern world  
  • Throughout history / Ever since I was a child  

  

Stylistic Choices  

Depending on your audience (academic, business, entertainment), your medium (essays, reports, emails, blog posts), and your natural voice, your choices here may vary.   

Writing in the Second Person  

The use of the word “you” as the subject, or main character, is generally not an academic voice. It is used primarily in fiction or in directions.   

  • I am going to use it all the time. I will be giving you direction.  
  • If “you” is part of a direct quote, then include it.   
  • Have you ever seen a rhetorical question? Now you have. It will often require the word “you.”   

The use of the word “you” puts the reader in the action. Doing so may alienate your reader. For example:   

  • “As a parent, when you punish, you need to know how to be consistent.”   

That may be true, but not all of your readers are parents. You may have alienated anyone who is not a parent.   

Other options to consider:  

  • Writing in the first person: “As I parent, when I punish, I know it is important to remain consistent.” 
  • Writing in the third person: “When punishing, parents need to remain consistent.”  

And, But and Or  

In academic writing, “And,” “But,” or “Or,” are used to combine two independent clauses into one complete sentence. When you use one at the beginning of a sentence, you are not using them the way they were originally intended.  

And I do it all the time. Some of my favorite authors – Shakespeare, F. Scott Fitzgerald – do so as well. But not without reason, and not without restraint.   

Em Dash   

Also know as the long dash (‘–’). Discouraged in academic and technical writing as it is, effectively, a non-specific pause and/or parenthetical. The audience has to infer from the text if it represents a comma, colon, semicolon, period, or parenthesis, and how important the information is to understanding your work. Some even consider it a telltale sign of Generative AI writing.  

And I do it all the time. Some of my favorite authors – Ray Bradbury, Charlie LeDuff – do so as well. Again: be intentional. Do it for a reason, use it sparingly.   

Parentheses  

Things in parentheses are, by definition, not actually part of the sentence. If your reader skipped the content in parenthesis, the sentence should still be clear. If the information is important, include it in the sentence. If not, try to exclude it. 

In general, I expect to see parenthesis in three scenarios:  

  1. An in-text citation: ...(last name, page).  
  1. Defining an abbreviation: ...Siena Heights University (SHU).  
  1. Providing a brief example: ...the long dash (‘–’).  

Works Cited vs Bibliography  

  • Works Cited: the MLA standard. 
    • Includes only the sources cited in the work. 
    • Alphabetized by authors last name, or title of reference if no author is credited. 
  • Bibliography: the Chicago style standard, also used by other citation styles. 
    • Includes all sources consulted, whether they were cited or not. 
    • Alphabetized by authors last name, or title of reference if no author is credited. 

As we are following the MLA citation standards, the Works Cited list/page is what's required. What matters is that you cite the sources you reference. Sources should be listed in alphabetical order, with the exception of "co-creators." Those should be listed last, and Generative AI co-creators should be the very last source. Common Grammatical Errors  

Subject/Verb Agreement  

Verbs must agree with their (not there or they’re) subjects. This seems to break down when the subject of the sentence is some distance from the verb.  

Which vs That  

“Which” is non-restrictive and “that” is restrictive. The general rule is that if the sentence would still make sense, use a comma and use which. If the information makes no sense without the qualifier, do not use a comma, and use that. For example:   

  • Lemurs, which hail from Madagascar, are my favorite primates. Lemurs that steal are bad.  
  • I am a vegetarian, which means I do not eat any meat. Food that once had a face is not for me.  

Then vs Than  

Then is a matter of time. Than is a matter of degree.   

  • Do this, then that.  
  • I was happier back then.  
  • He is bigger than she is.  
  • He is happier than I am.

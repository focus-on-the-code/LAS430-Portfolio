Priority 2: Prof. T's Generative AI Use Policy

Course artifact — adapted for public review from Canvas export

The TLDR: Generative AI (GenAI, AI) is allowed in this class, but only with responsibility and transparency. My expectations for its use are summarized below. You are expected to know, and apply this policy.

Failure to adhere to policy will be considered academic dishonesty, and subject to disciplinary action as per the Student Code of Conduct.  

Continue reading the full document below. You will not be successful if you stop at this summary.   

  • Use it as a partner, not a substitute. AI can help refine ideas, not replace your thinking. 
  • Always cite it. Treat AI output like a source, follow MLA citation format, and show me when you’ve used it. 
  • Be transparent. Include a full transcript of prompts and outputs with your work. 
  • Think first, and last: Your original ideas come first. Your review and final edit of AI output is critical.

The full Generative AI Use Policy below explains: 

  • University-level rules you must also follow. 
  • Academic dishonesty with AI. 
  • Examples of acceptable use cases, and risks to avoid. 
  • MLA citation guidance and examples. 

Philosophy & Context  

Humans have always created tools to extend our capabilities. The printing press, to share our ideas broadly.  Planes, trains, and automobiles to take us further, faster. War machines to capture resources, and do harm.   

Generative AI (GenAI, AI, Large Language Models or LLMs) is the latest tool we've created. The breadth, and speed of its adoption is unprecedented. Like all tools, it can help us improve ourselves, our work, and our communities. It can also do harm, by accident and by choice. I believe the choice is ours to make. To make an informed choice, we all must learn to use it through low-stakes experiments. This class is the best place I can think to experiment.  

I view GenAI as a partner for thinking, learning, and creating. A tool to help you work smarter, think deeper. The underlying content, the source material, should be your original thoughts and ideas. AI should be used to help you expand, refine, challenge, and express your ideas, in ways aligned with your intent. This is how I use these tools, and how I expect you to use these tools, when you chose to do so.   

AI is not a substitute for intellectual effort. AI is not a shortcut. The goal is not to have AI read, write, and think for you. That's your job. That's what you signed up for when you made the choice to be here. Generative AI is powerful tool, but your education is about developing your own skills. Use these tools wisely, transparently, and within policy. Respect the you who made the choice for higher education. Honor your commitment to the struggle that is required to learn and grow.   

Start there, and you will be successful no matter what tools you use.   

-Prof. T  

Siena Heights University Policy  

Siena Heights University does not ban the use of Generative AI. Instead, it treats AI like any other source. Essentially:   

  • Use it ethically.  
  • Cite it accurately.  
  • Do not allow it to replace your own thinking or judgment.  

Misrepresentation of AI-generated material as entirely your own work constitutes academic dishonesty under the Student Code of Conduct. Use of AI tools to create and disseminate material that is obscene, harassing, threatening, or disruptive violates all related clauses under the Student Code of Conduct. Violation of the code of conduct is subject to disciplinary action, as per the code (Siena Heights Student Code of Conduct).  

Please understand: this is not a threat. My goal is to be clear and transparent, so you can make an informed choice when, and how, you use these tools.  

  

Class Rules (Non-Negotiable)  

  1. Cite AI Use
  • Treat AI output as source material.  
  • Follow MLA guidelines for quoting or paraphrasing AI output, naming the tool and company, and noting the date used. For example:   
    • Direct quote, in-text citation: “The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog” (ChatGPT).  
    • Paraphrasing, in-text citation:   
      • Non-narrative citation: We discussed pangrams, and the well known example involving a fox and a dog (ChatGPT).  
      • Narrative citation: I researched pangrams, with ChatGPT’s assistance, and the well known example involving a fox and a dog.  
    • Works Cited: follow guidelines for personal communication/digital conversation   
      • Structure: AI Tool. Description of the conversation. Company, Date  
      • Example: ChatGPT. Conversation about pangrams. OpenAI, 12 Aug. 2025  

Unless otherwise stated in the instructions, when editing documents with AI tools (e.g.: papers, discussion posts, emails), and/or output other than text (e.g.: charts, images, datasets, code, programs), add the following to the Works Cited list/page, or clearly displayed in the output: 

Co-created with [AI Tool, Company].  

  1. Attach the Full Transcript
  • When using AI tools for any assignment, submit the full transcript of your work with AI along with your assignment (e.g.: your prompts, and AI responses/outputs, the full “conversation”).   
  • This transparency allows me to give targeted feedback, and helps us all learn from each other.  

How to Attach the Full Transcript

  1. Original Thinking First and Last 
  • AI use must supplement, not replace, your own analysis, decision-making, and writing.  
  • Start with your ideas and drafts.
  • End with your detailed review and edits of all AI output. You are responsible for the quality of work you submit.
  • Copy-paste submissions without added personal input are prohibited, and will be considered academic dishonesty, and a violation of the Student Code of Conduct.   

  

Guidelines & Use Cases  

These are best practices and common use cases to help you stay within policy:  

  • Your unique thoughts and ideas are the source material: the notes you add for context, the material you select for reference, the details of your prompts and conversations with AI. Start with your first draft, with what you think. Use the tools to refine your ideas.   
  • Share how you're using AI in the Chit-Chat channel - new use cases, tools, and learning resources are welcome, so long as they follow the rules. We’re here to learn and experiment together.   
  • Common Use Cases:   
    • AI as a thought partner: brainstorming, idea expansion, problem-solving.  
    • AI as an editor: clarifying, organizing, outlining, proofreading.  
    • Double-check your work meets the assignment requirements.  
    • Chat with the text to deepen your engagement.   

Unless otherwise stated in the instructions, you may chose to use AI, or not. Some assignments may require GenAI use. All others are optional.  

  

Risks & Considerations  

Generative AI is powerful. Like all tools and technology, its use comes with risks. This is not a reason to avoid using AI, but a reason to be informed, and understand risks you are accepting. These risks are best described in a New York Times opinion article: A.I. Is Fueling a ‘Poverty of Imagination.’ Here’s How We Can Fix It  

  1. Educational risks
  • Can short-circuit the struggle and practice that build deep learning and critical thinking.  
  • Inherent to their design, LLMs may produce shallow, generic responses, as well as false or fabricated information (erroneously dubbed, “hallucinations”).   
  1. Social & emotional risks
  • Overuse can erode the pride and resilience that come from mastering a skill.  
  • AI’s human-like tone can lead to misplaced trust.  
  • Peer pressure and hype may push adoption without reflection.  
  1. Technical & privacy risks
  • Inputting your work into AI tools can expose personal or academic data.  
  • Responsibility for errors often falls on the user, with little-to-no accountability from providers.  
  • The technology is moving faster than meaningful oversight or regulation.
